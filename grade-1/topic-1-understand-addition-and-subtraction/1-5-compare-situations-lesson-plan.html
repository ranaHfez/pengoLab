enVision Mathematics — Grade 1

Topic 1, Lesson 5: Compare Situations

Full Interactive Lesson Plan

📋 Lesson Overview
Grade 1
Topic 1 — Understand Addition and Subtraction
Lesson 5 — Compare Situations
Standard 1.OA.A.1 — Use addition and subtraction within 20 to solve word problems involving situations of adding to, taking from, putting together, taking apart, and comparing, with unknowns in all positions
Duration 45–50 minutes
Materials Two-color counters or connecting cubes (10–20 per student), whiteboard/chart paper, number cards 0–10, ten-frames (optional), student math journals, "Compare Bars" mat (two connected rectangles)

Learning Objective

Students will represent and solve compare problems — finding "how many more" or "how many fewer" one group has than another — using objects, drawings, and the language more than / fewer than.

Key Vocabulary

  • compare — to look at two groups and decide which has more or fewer
  • more — a greater amount
  • fewer — a smaller amount
  • the same as / equal to

1️⃣ Warm-Up Game (8–10 min): "More or Fewer? Stand Up!"

Purpose: Activate comparison thinking using real groups of students/objects before moving to abstract representations.

Setup: No materials needed — uses students themselves as the "objects" to compare.

How to Play:

  1. Split the class into two groups by a quick, visible trait (e.g., "Everyone wearing sneakers" vs. "Everyone wearing other shoes"), and have each group line up.
  2. The class counts each line aloud together.
  3. Ask: "Which line has more? Which line has fewer? How many more?"
  4. Call one student to walk along both lines and physically match one-to-one (hold hands / point) with a partner in the other line — the unmatched students show the "how many more."
  5. Repeat with 3–4 new groupings (e.g., "short sleeves vs. long sleeves," "boys vs. girls," "backpack vs. no backpack").

Book Connection: This mirrors the enVision "Solve & Share" opener style, where students act out comparisons before using counters — matching one-to-one is exactly how the lesson's visual model (connecting cubes lined up in rows) works.

Teacher Prompt Script:

"When we match people one-to-one, whoever doesn't have a partner tells us how many more. Let's count together: 1, 2, 3 extra friends — that group has 3 more!"

2️⃣ Solve & Share — Book Example (10 min)

Introduce the lesson's anchor problem (typical enVision Topic 1 Lesson 5 context — adjust names/numbers to match your exact book page if different):

"Jackson has 4 red apples. Emma has 6 green apples. How many more apples does Emma have than Jackson?"

Guided Modeling Steps:

  1. Give each student 4 red counters and 6 green (or two colors of cubes).
  2. Have students line the two groups up directly under each other on their Compare Bars mat (two stacked rows), matching one-to-one.
  3. Circle/point to the "extra" cubes with no match.
  4. Model the sentence frame:
  • "Emma has ___ more apples than Jackson."
  • "Jackson has ___ fewer apples than Emma."
  1. Write the matching equation together: 4 + 2 = 6 or 6 − 4 = 2, showing that comparison problems connect to both addition and subtraction.

Second Book-Style Example (Equal Groups):

"Mia has 5 stickers. Sam has 5 stickers too. Compare the groups." Use this to introduce "the same as"/"equal" when there is no difference — an important non-example students often miss.

3️⃣ Visual Learning Bridge — "I Do / We Do" (8 min)

Draw two compare bars on the board (one long bar, one short bar, stacked):

Emma:  🟩🟩🟩🟩🟩🟩
Jackson: 🟥🟥🟥🟥

Think-aloud:

"I line up the bars. I see Emma's bar is longer. I count the extra squares that stick out — 1, 2. Emma has 2 more than Jackson."

We Do: Give a new pair of numbers (e.g., 7 and 3) and have students build both bars with cubes at their desks while you circulate and check matching.

4️⃣ Practicing Games (Independent / Partner Practice, 15 min)

🎲 Game 1: "Compare & Cover" (Partner Game)

Materials: Two-color counters, two small cups, a "Compare Bars" recording mat per pair.

How to Play:

  1. Partner A scoops a small handful of counters and spills them; count how many landed red side up.
  2. Partner B does the same, counting yellow side up.
  3. Both partners build their number as a bar of cubes/counters on the shared mat, lined up under each other.
  4. Together they decide: Who has more? How many more? How many fewer does the other partner have?
  5. Record the sentence and equation in a math journal: "___ has ___ more than ___." and the number sentence.
  6. Play 4–5 rounds, switching who reports first each time.

🃏 Game 2: "Who Has More?" (Card Comparison Game)

Materials: Number cards 0–10 (two sets, shuffled and split between partners), counters.

How to Play:

  1. Each partner flips one card at the same time.
  2. Both build that many cubes in a tower.
  3. Partners compare towers by standing them side-by-side.
  4. Whoever has more says: "I have ___ more than you!" and keeps both cards.
  5. If equal, both call "Match!" and cards go to a discard pile.
  6. Play until cards run out; whoever collected more card pairs wins.

(This directly practices the "more/fewer/equal" vocabulary in a fast, repeatable format matching the book's fluency-building intent.)

🏁 Game 3: "Race to Compare" (Whole-Class or Small-Group Board Game)

Materials: A simple path board (butcher paper with 20 numbered spaces), one die, one game piece per player, counters.

How to Play:

  1. Players take turns rolling the die and moving that many spaces.
  2. Before moving, the player must compare their current space number to the space they'll land on, saying: "I'm moving from ___ to ___, so I'll have ___ more steps than before" (reinforces comparison language in context, not just with objects).
  3. First to reach space 20 wins.

5️⃣ Quick Check / Formative Assessment (5 min)

Show two bars/groups (e.g., 8 blue counters, 5 orange counters) on the board. Students independently write on a whiteboard or exit ticket:

  1. Which group has more?
  2. How many more?
  3. Complete: "___ has ___ fewer than ___."
  4. Write the matching equation.

Look-fors: Correct one-to-one matching, correct use of more/fewer, and a matching addition or subtraction equation.

6️⃣ Closing / Wrap-Up (3–5 min)

Gather students and ask:

"Today we compared groups. What word do we use when one group has a bigger number? What word do we use when it has a smaller number? How do we find out how many more or fewer?"

Have 1–2 students share their exit ticket reasoning aloud.

🌟 Differentiation

Support Needed Adjustment
Struggling learners Use numbers within 5; provide a pre-drawn ten-frame or matched dot grid for physical matching
On-level Use numbers within 10, as modeled above
Advanced learners Extend to numbers within 20; add a third group and ask "which two groups are closest in number?"
English Language Learners Pair vocabulary cards (more, fewer, same) with visuals; sentence frames stay posted throughout
